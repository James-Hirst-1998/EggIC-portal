How it reaches an answer

What it learned from

13,054 photographs of egg cases, wet and dry, whole and broken. The model was not told what a keel or a horn is; it was shown many examples of each species and worked out for itself which differences matter. The nine species are unevenly represented, from 462 Cuckoo Ray photographs to 1,911 Blonde Ray, and the training corrects for that imbalance so the rarer species are not simply ignored.

Reading the result

Every answer comes with a confidence: the model's own estimate of how likely its first choice is to be right. The confidences across all nine species always add up to 100%, so a high number for one species necessarily means low numbers elsewhere.

Accept a call at 87.5% or above. Below that, treat it as a shortlist, not an identification.

Confidence is doing real work here: the more certain the model says it is, the more often it is right. 87.5% sits just clear of the first mistake: the most confident wrong answer in those 224 photographs came in at 83.5%, four points below the line. Everything the tool accepted, it got right — 133 photographs, three in every five.

The mistakes it did make are mostly of a mild kind. Of the 29 wrong answers, 28 were one Raja taken for another, most often a Thornback called a Blonde. Only one crossed genus, and that came in at 50% confidence, nowhere near the line.

When it is unsure

  • An egg case outside the nine species. The model has no way to answer "none of these", so it will still name one of the nine. Usually the confidence drops and the remaining probability spreads across several species — but not always. Of 19 photographs of untaught species we tested, 5 still cleared 87.5% under the wrong name. This is the tool's weakest point, and the confidence number will not warn you about it. If you have reason to think the case is something else, trust that over the number.
  • More than one egg case in the frame. The model answers for the photograph as a whole, not for each case in it. Several cases together — particularly of different species — pull the confidence apart between them. Photograph one case at a time.
  • An unclear photograph. Badly worn, broken, half-buried or very distant cases usually push the confidence down and spread it across several species. A low, flat spread is the signal to treat the answer as a shortlist.
  • Two similar candidates. Where the model is torn between close relatives you will see two species sharing most of the confidence. That is genuinely useful — it narrows the decision to a pair you can then separate by eye.

Honest limits

  • The accuracy figures come from 224 held-out photographs. That is a small sample, and the percentages should be read as indicative rather than precise.
  • The 87.5% line was chosen using those same 224 photographs, so "none of them wrong" is the best case rather than a promise. On photographs we have not seen, the honest reading is at least 97% right above the line.
  • Those photographs were supplied separately and were never used in training, so the figures are a genuine test rather than a rehearsal. They are not, however, public submissions. Performance on photographs taken by the public, in conditions we have not seen, may be lower — establishing that is what this round of testing is for.
  • The model sees only the photograph. It does not know where or when the case was found, and location alone would rule out some species immediately.
  • It cannot say "this is not an egg case". Photograph anything else and it will still return nine numbers.
